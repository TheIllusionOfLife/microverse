A result from the Microverse · reported honestly

Can AI agents learn to divide labor?

Yes — but only once we got the economics right. A tiny village of AI minds on one laptop kept doing the same thing, over and over. Then we changed one rule, and they started specializing.

runs on one laptop $0 cloud cost same move ~90% → divided labor every number sourced
01 / 10The setup

A tiny village of AI minds

The Microverse is a small simulated world that runs entirely on one laptop. A handful of AI agents live inside it. Each one, on its turn, picks one action from a short menu of six things to do. There is no cloud, no API bill: every decision is a local call to one small open model (gemma4:26b), and every choice is written to a local log you can audit.

Run conditions, in plain terms: one model (gemma4:26b), 3,000 turns per run, and each result repeated on three independent random starts (we call them seeds) so a single lucky run can't carry the story. "Dividing labor" just means different residents reliably pick different actions.

02 / 10The problem

Left alone, everyone does the same thing

With no pressure to do otherwise, the agents pile onto one safe, generic action — contribute (add a bit to a shared work-in-progress). In the two-resident village, roughly 9 out of every 10 actions were the same verb. That is a monoculture: a society where everyone behaves identically and nobody specializes.audit · ADR 0013

Why does this happen? The generic action is always "good enough." With nothing making it costly, the smart move for every agent, every turn, is the same default — so the village collapses into sameness.

03 / 10The change, seen

Watch the village divide its labor

Here is the heart of it. The idea is simple: make the easy default action cost more energy. When contribute gets expensive, each agent drifts to the one action that's cheap for them — the artisan to craft, the scholar to study, the newcomer to travel. 04 / 10Measuring it

Putting a number on "divided labor"

To judge this fairly we need one number for "how differently are the residents behaving?" Higher means more divided labor. We drew a pass line at 0.25 and — this matters — locked it in writing before looking at any of these results.ADR 0016 The chart shows what raising the dose does, for two-resident and three-resident villages alike.

What is this number, exactly?

It's the average pairwise Jensen–Shannon divergence between residents' action mixes (mean_pairwise_jsd): 0 means everyone behaves identically, 1 means completely separate roles. The 0.25 line was calibrated on the two-resident case and then inherited unchanged for larger villages, so a bigger roster can't quietly lower the bar. Each bar is the average of three fresh-seed runs; the dots are the individual seeds, so you can see the spread, not just the average. The two-resident doses (bal@22 / bal@30) come from the held-out replication;ADR 0014 the three-resident doses from the roster and dose-rescue studies.ADR 0015

05 / 10Stress tests

Does it survive scrutiny?

A result is only worth reporting if you try hard to break it. So we did. The first three-resident test failed. Persona prompting didn't fix it. Re-weighting the costs alone didn't fix it. Changing the economic pressure did. Here is that trail, wins and dead-ends alike — click any row for detail.

Each verdict is a real sweep with its own pre-registered rule. "Backfired" and "wash" are genuine negative results — kept on the record, not quietly dropped.

06 / 10The resolution

The failure wasn't the design — it was the dose

When a third resident joins, each agent gets the spotlight less often, so the same cost spread over more residents pushes each one less. The fix wasn't a cleverer design; it was simply matching the dose to the bigger village (raising the contribute cost from 30 to 42). Do that, and the three-resident village clears the line: divergence rises from 0.21 to 0.31, passing on all three fresh seeds.ADR 0018

An honest bound: this is one rescued three-resident village, not a proven law. It's good evidence for a simple "dilution" explanation — bigger village needs a bigger dose — and it cleared the differentiation bar on all three seeds and the stricter role-stability check on two of three. It is not yet shown to hold for much larger populations, other action menus, or other models.

07 / 10Why trust it

How we know it's real

The discipline behind the result matters as much as the result: we predicted, locked it in, and checked on runs we'd never seen.

Decided in advance

The pass line and the rules were written to the repository before each sweep ran, so nothing could be tuned after seeing the answer.

Held-out seeds

The two-resident pass was re-tested on three fresh random starts it had never seen, and held — divergence 0.34 / 0.37 / 0.39.ADR 0014

Mechanism, not luck

An offline audit confirms the cost signal is what moves the agents — when the scarcity hint names a verb, the model obeys it, run after run.ADR 0013

08 / 10What it means

What this is — and what it isn't

This is a real, if bounded, finding about how small AI societies behave: left to themselves they collapse into sameness, and a simple economic pressure — properly dosed — pushes them apart into roles. It's fully local, costs nothing to run, and every number is logged and auditable.

Being clear about scope: this is a discovery about agent behavior, not energy or cures for the real world. We've shown the agents divide labor. The open question is whether that specialization produces work that's actually good — and to measure that, we'd need an outside judge. That's exactly where this points next.
09 / 10What's next

Future directions

The real-output path

from "different" to "good"

Today the village's own Trader decides what's valuable — a judge from inside the world. The next leap is an external oracle: let agents produce things the outside world can score on its own — code that passes tests, a proof a checker verifies, a forecast graded against held-out data. Then "discovery" stops being a vibe and becomes a measurable, externally-verifiable output.

Test the dilution idea

does the dose really scale?

The dose-rescue is one datapoint. The clean test: add a fourth resident with a fourth specialty and see whether a still-larger dose keeps the labor divided — the direct prediction of the dilution explanation. Honest caveat: the fourth available action (speak) is the weakest specialty, and four is the practical ceiling of this action menu.
